2. Undoing an operation

What you will be able to do

  • Match each operation with the one that undoes it.
  • Find cube roots of perfect cubes.
  • Undo a two-step rule in reverse order.

Every operation has a partner

Some operations reverse each other. If you do one and then the other, you are back where you started. These pairs are called inverse operations.

Cubes and cube roots

To cube a number is to use it as a factor three times: 2^3 = 2 * 2 * 2 = 8 2 cubed equals 2 times 2 times 2 equals 8 . The cube root goes backward. It asks "what number cubed gives this?" The cube root of 8 is 2. Cube roots work for negative numbers too: the cube root of -8 negative 8 is -2 negative 2 , because (-2)(-2)(-2) = -8 open parenthesis negative 2 close parenthesis open parenthesis negative 2 close parenthesis open parenthesis negative 2 close parenthesis equals negative 8 . A few perfect cubes are worth knowing: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125.

Undoing more than one step

When a rule has several steps, undo them in the reverse order, last step first. Suppose a rule says "multiply by 4, then add 3." Starting with 5 gives 20, then 23. To get back from 23, first undo the adding (23 - 3 = 20 23 minus 3 equals 20 ), then undo the multiplying (20/4 = 5 20 over 4 equals 5 ).

Why reverse order. In the morning you put on socks, then shoes. At night you take off the shoes first, then the socks. The last thing done is the first thing undone. It is the same with operations. The most recent one is on the "outside," so it has to come off first.

This is exactly what an inverse function does. It takes an output and walks backward through the steps to recover the input.

Walk backward

A rule cubes a number and then subtracts 5. The result is 22. What was the starting number?

  1. 22 + 5 = 27 22 plus 5 equals 27 Undo the last step first. The rule subtracted 5, so add 5.
  2. cube root of 27 = 3 cube root of 27 equals 3 Then undo the cube.

Answer: 3. Check: 3^3 = 27 3 cubed equals 27 , and 27 - 5 = 22 27 minus 5 equals 22 .

3. Solving for y

What you will be able to do

  • Peel operations off y in reverse order.
  • Write a whole side over a number.
  • Use a cube root to undo a cube.

Same skill, different letter

The lecture's method for finding an inverse ends with "solve for y." At that point the equation has x alone on one side and y buried on the other, such as x = 3y + 6 x equals 3 y plus 6 . This looks backward, but it is an ordinary solving problem. Treat x as if it were a number and peel the operations off y, last one first.

x = 3y + 6 x equals 3 y plus 6 x - 6 = 3y (subtract 6 from both sides) x minus 6 equals 3 y open parenthesis subtract 6 from both sides close parenthesis (x - 6)/3 = y (divide both sides by 3) open parenthesis x minus 6 close parenthesis over 3 equals y open parenthesis divide both sides by 3 close parenthesis

Divide the whole side

When you divide by 3, the entire left side is divided. Write it as one fraction with the whole expression on top: (x - 6 x minus 6 )/3. Writing x - 6/3 x minus 6 over 3 would divide only the 6, which is a different thing. When you type the answer, put the top in parentheses.

Why the order is the same as before. Read x = 3y + 6 x equals 3 y plus 6 as a story about y: "y was multiplied by 3, then 6 was added, and the result is x." To recover y, undo the adding first and then the multiplying, just as in the last section. The only difference is that the result is x, a letter, so the answer is a formula.

When y is cubed

If y has a power of 3, get the y^3 y cubed term alone first. Then take the cube root of both sides. Unlike a square root, a cube root gives only one answer, so there is no plus-or-minus.

Solve with a cube

Solve x = y^3 + 4 x equals y cubed plus 4 for y.

  1. x - 4 = y^3 x minus 4 equals y cubed Subtract 4 from both sides. Now the cube is alone.
  2. cbrt(x - 4) = y the cube root of x minus 4, end root, equals y Take the cube root of both sides. The root covers the whole side.

Answer: y = cbrt(x - 4 x minus 4 )

When y is inside a fraction

If the whole right side is divided by a number, multiply both sides by that number first.

x = (y - 5)/2 x equals open parenthesis y minus 5 close parenthesis over 2 2x = y - 5 (multiply both sides by 2) 2 x equals y minus 5 open parenthesis multiply both sides by 2 close parenthesis 2x + 5 = y (add 5 to both sides) 2 x plus 5 equals y open parenthesis add 5 to both sides close parenthesis

4. Simplifying when operations cancel

What you will be able to do

  • Cancel a number with a matching denominator.
  • Combine opposites before dividing.
  • Cancel a cube with a cube root.

What the check looks like

The lecture checks two functions by putting one inside the other and simplifying. If they really are inverses, everything cancels and only x is left. The skill is seeing which pieces cancel, and why. There are three patterns.

Pattern 1: multiplying a fraction by its own bottom

A number times a fraction with that same number on the bottom cancels the pair. What is left is the top.

3 * (x - 6)/3 = x - 6 3 times open parenthesis x minus 6 close parenthesis over 3 equals x minus 6

The 3 outside and the 3 underneath divide to 1. Keep the top together as a group until the cancelling is done.

Pattern 2: opposites on top of a fraction

Combine like terms on top first. Opposites add to zero. Then divide.

(3x + 6 - 6)/3 = (3x)/3 = x open parenthesis 3 x plus 6 minus 6 close parenthesis over 3 equals open parenthesis 3 x close parenthesis over 3 equals x

Pattern 3: a cube and a cube root

Cubing and taking a cube root undo each other, in either order. Whatever was inside comes back out unchanged.

(cbrt(x - 4))^3 = x - 4 open parenthesis the cube root of x minus 4, end root, close parenthesis cubed equals x minus 4 cbrt(x^3) = x the cube root of x cubed, end root, equals x
Why everything disappears. These are the inverse operations from the first section, now applied to expressions. Multiplying by 3 and dividing by 3 undo each other. Adding 6 and subtracting 6 undo each other. Cubing and cube-rooting undo each other. When two functions are inverses, each step of one is met by the undoing step of the other, so the whole thing collapses to x.

A full simplification

Simplify 3 * (x - 6 x minus 6 )/3 + 6.

  1. (x - 6) + 6 open parenthesis x minus 6 close parenthesis plus 6 The 3 outside cancels the 3 underneath. The top is left.
  2. x-6 + 6 = 0 negative 6 plus 6 equals 0 .

Answer: x

A common wrong turn is to cancel the 3 with only part of the top, or to distribute the 3 and forget the bottom. If you prefer to distribute, do it fully: (3x - 18 3 x minus 18 )/3 = x - 6 x minus 6 . Both routes give the same result.

5. Horizontal lines and counting crossings

What you will be able to do

  • Tell a horizontal line from a vertical line.
  • Count how many times a horizontal line crosses a graph.
  • Connect a crossing with an input that gives that output.

What a horizontal line is

A horizontal line is flat. It runs left and right. Every point on it has the same height, so its equation is y = a y equals a number. The line y = 1 y equals 1 is all the points with height 1. A vertical line runs up and down, and its equation is x = a x equals a number. Section 2.1 used vertical lines. This section uses horizontal ones, so keep the two straight.

What a crossing means

Draw the horizontal line y = 1 y equals 1 across a graph. Each place it meets the graph is a point with height 1, which means an input whose output is 1. So counting the crossings counts how many different inputs give the output 1.

On Graph A the line at height 1 crosses twice, at x = -2 x equals negative 2 and at x = 2 x equals 2 . Two different inputs give the same output. That is easy to confirm with arithmetic: (-2)^2 - 3 = 1 open parenthesis negative 2 close parenthesis squared minus 3 equals 1 and (2)^2 - 3 = 1 open parenthesis 2 close parenthesis squared minus 3 equals 1 .

Why crossings matter for undoing. An inverse has to walk backward from an output to the input it came from. If the output 1 came from both -2 negative 2 and 2, there is no way to know which one to go back to. That is the problem the lecture's test is built to catch. A graph where no horizontal line ever crosses twice never has this problem.

On Graph B, any horizontal line you draw meets the line exactly once. Each output comes from one input only.

Try several heights

One horizontal line is not enough to judge a graph. On Graph A, a line at height -3 negative 3 touches only once, at the bottom of the U. A line at height -5 negative 5 does not touch at all. The question is whether any horizontal line crosses more than once. Slide the line up and down in your mind and look for the worst case.
