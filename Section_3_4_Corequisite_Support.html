1. Start here

What you will be able to do

  • List every factor of a whole number.
  • Write plus-or-minus lists and count what is in them.
  • Build fractions from two lists, reduce them, and drop repeats.
  • Identify the leading coefficient and the constant term.
  • Read a table of values to find zeros.
  • Simplify an answer from the quadratic formula.

Section 3.4 finds the zeros of a polynomial. The lecture teaches the Rational Zero Theorem, which produces a list of candidates, and then uses a Desmos table to test them. The theorem sounds advanced, but carrying it out is mostly arithmetic from much earlier: listing the factors of a whole number, writing plus-or-minus pairs, building and reducing fractions, picking out the first and last numbers of a polynomial, and reading a table to see which inputs give zero. This page practices those, plus the square-root simplifying needed when the last zeros come from the quadratic formula.

Before you begin. The notes for this section open with a warning that the method differs from the e-text, which can be unsettling. The steps themselves are small and repetitive. If listing factors or reducing fractions feels slow, that usually comes from time away from math, not from a lack of ability. Nothing here is graded and nobody sees your answers. Take breaks whenever you want. Your progress saves on this device.
How to enter answers. When a question asks for a list, separate the numbers with commas in any order. Write fractions with a slash and reduce them. Use +- for plus or minus, like 3+-sqrt(5) 3 plus or minus the square root of 5 .

2. Factors of a whole number

What you will be able to do

  • Decide whether one number is a factor of another.
  • List every factor by working in pairs.
  • Include 1 and the number itself.

What a factor is

A factor of a whole number is a whole number that divides it evenly, with no remainder. 3 is a factor of 12 because 12/3 = 4 12 over 3 equals 4 exactly. 5 is not a factor of 12 because 12/5 leaves a remainder. The Rational Zero Theorem begins by listing all the factors of two numbers, so a complete list matters. One missed factor means missed candidates.

Find factors in pairs

Factors come in pairs that multiply to the number. Start at 1 and work upward. For each number that divides evenly, write it and its partner. Stop when the pairs start to repeat.

Factors of 121 * 12 1 times 12 2 * 6 2 times 6 3 * 4 3 times 4 (4 * 3 would repeat, so stop) open parenthesis 4 times 3 would repeat, so stop close parenthesis List: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
Why pairs. Every factor has a partner, because if 3 goes into 12 four times, then 4 goes into 12 three times. Working in pairs means you only have to test numbers up to about the square root of the number. Past that point the small partner would already have been found. It also guarantees you do not forget the large factors, which are the ones people usually miss.

Always include 1 and the number itself

1 divides everything, and every number divides itself. So the list always starts with 1 and ends with the number. Some numbers have no other factors. Those are called prime. The factors of 7 are only 1 and 7.

Quick tests for small divisors

  • 2 divides a number that ends in 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8.
  • 3 divides a number whose digits add up to a multiple of 3. For 24: 2 + 4 = 6 2 plus 4 equals 6 , so 3 divides 24.
  • 5 divides a number that ends in 0 or 5.

A longer list

List all the factors of 24.

  1. 1 * 24 1 times 24 1 always works.
  2. 2 * 12 2 times 12 24 is even.
  3. 3 * 8 3 times 8 The digits add to 6.
  4. 4 * 6 4 times 6 24/4 = 6 24 over 4 equals 6 .
  5. 5 does not divide 24. The next would be 6, already found.Stop.

Factors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24

3. Plus-or-minus lists and building fractions

What you will be able to do

  • Expand a ± list.
  • Form every top over every bottom.
  • Reduce and remove repeats.

Each factor counts twice

A zero of a polynomial can be positive or negative. So the lecture writes each factor with a plus-or-minus sign in front. The symbol ± is shorthand for two numbers.

±3 means 3 and -3 plus or minus 3 means 3 and negative 3 ±1, ±2 means 1, -1, 2, -2 plus or minus 1, plus or minus 2 means 1, negative 1, 2, negative 2

A list written with ± contains twice as many numbers as it appears to. ±1 plus or minus 1 , ±2 plus or minus 2 , ±4 plus or minus 4 is six numbers.

Every top over every bottom

The theorem then forms fractions: each number from the first list on top, over each number from the second list on the bottom. Be systematic so none is missed. Take the first bottom and put every top over it. Then take the next bottom and do it again.

tops: 1, 3 bottoms: 1, 2over 1: 1/1, 3/1 over 1 : 1 over 1, 3 over 1 over 2: 1/2, 3/2 over 2 : 1 over 2, 3 over 2

Reduce, and convert anything over 1

A fraction over 1 is a whole number: 3/1 = 3 3 over 1 equals 3 . Any other fraction should be reduced by dividing top and bottom by the same number.

4/2 = 2 4 over 2 equals 2 2/4 = 1/2 2 over 4 equals 1 over 2 6/4 = 3/2 6 over 4 equals 3 over 2

Drop the repeats

After reducing, some fractions turn out to be numbers already on the list. Write each value only once.

Build the full list

Tops: 1, 2. Bottoms: 1, 2. List every different value, then attach ±.

  1. over 1: 1/1 = 1, 2/1 = 2 over 1 : 1 over 1 equals 1, 2 over 1 equals 2 Every top over the first bottom.
  2. over 2: 1/2, 2/2 = 1 over 2 : 1 over 2, 2 over 2 equals 1 Every top over the second bottom. 2/2 reduces to 1.
  3. 1, 2, 1/2 1, 2, 1 over 2 The value 1 appeared twice. Keep it once.
  4. ±1, ±2, ±1/2 plus or minus 1, plus or minus 2, plus or minus 1 over 2 Attach the plus-or-minus.

Answer: ±1 plus or minus 1 , ±2 plus or minus 2 , ±1/2. That is six candidates.

Why reduce before listing. Two fractions that reduce to the same thing are the same number, so testing both would be wasted effort. 2/2 and 1/1 are both just 1. Reducing first is what lets you see the repeats. The lecture's own example has dozens of fractions before reducing, so a steady routine (one bottom at a time, reduce, cross off repeats) is worth more here than speed.

4. Leading coefficient and constant term

What you will be able to do

  • Find the leading coefficient and constant term in any order.
  • Read the subscript notation.
  • Use the degree as a limit on the number of zeros.

Two numbers drive the whole theorem

The Rational Zero Theorem uses only two numbers from the polynomial: the first and the last, once it is in descending order. Getting those two right is the entire setup.

  • The leading coefficient is the number in front of the term with the largest power.
  • The constant term is the number with no x.
3x^4 - 2x + 8 leading coefficient 3, constant term 8 3 x to the power 4 minus 2 x plus 8 leading coefficient 3, constant term 8

The symbols in the notes

The notes write the leading coefficient as a with a small n beside it and the constant term as a with a small 0 beside it. The small number is called a subscript. It is only a label that tells you which power the coefficient belongs to. It is not an exponent and it is not multiplied. So "a sub n" is the coefficient on the highest power, x^n x to the power n , and "a sub 0" is the coefficient on x^0 x to the power 0 , which is the constant.

Things that hide the two numbers

  • No number in front. In x^3 - 5x^2 + 6 x cubed minus 5 x squared plus 6 the leading coefficient is 1.
  • Out of order. In 4 - x^2 + 6x^3 4 minus x squared plus 6 x cubed the largest power is 3, so the leading coefficient is 6 and the constant term is 4.
  • Negative signs. In -2x^3 + 7x - 12 negative 2 x cubed plus 7 x minus 12 the constant term is -12 negative 12 . For listing factors, use the size, 12. The ± takes care of the sign.
Why first and last. The theorem says a rational zero p/q must have p dividing the constant term and q dividing the leading coefficient. You can see why with a small case. If 2x - 3 2 x minus 3 is a factor of a polynomial, its zero is 3/2. Multiplying the factors out, the 2 ends up inside the leading coefficient and the 3 ends up inside the constant term. So the bottom of a zero comes from the front number and the top comes from the back number.

Degree and the number of zeros

The notes also use the degree, the largest power, as a limit: a polynomial has at most that many zeros. A degree 3 polynomial has at most 3. Once you have found that many, you can stop testing.

Pick out the two numbers

For P(x) = 7x - 4x^3 + 9 P open parenthesis x close parenthesis equals 7 x minus 4 x cubed plus 9 , find the leading coefficient, the constant term, and the largest possible number of zeros.

  1. -4x^3 + 7x + 9 negative 4 x cubed plus 7 x plus 9 Write it in descending order.
  2. leading coefficient: -4 leading coefficient : negative 4 The number on x^3. x to the power 3 .
  3. constant term: 9The number with no x.
  4. at most 3 zerosThe degree is 3.

For listing factors, you would use 4 and 9.

5. Reading a table for zeros

What you will be able to do

  • Read a table for rows with output 0.
  • Evaluate at a fraction by hand.
  • Stop when the number of zeros reaches the degree.

What the Desmos table shows

After listing the candidates, the lecture types each one into a Desmos table. The table has two columns. The left column is the input x. The right column is the output P(x). You are looking for one thing: rows where the output is exactly 0. Those inputs are the zeros.

Why 0 is the number to look for. A zero is an input whose output is 0. On a graph, that is a point where the curve meets the x-axis, since the height there is 0. So the table and the graph tell the same story. If you graph the polynomial in Desmos, you can see roughly where it meets the x-axis and test those candidates first.

Close does not count

Only an output of exactly 0 marks a zero. An output like 2.5 or -2 negative 2 means that candidate is not a zero, even if it looks small.

6. Simplifying a quadratic formula answer

What you will be able to do

  • Simplify a square root by pulling out a perfect square.
  • Divide both terms of a quadratic formula answer.
  • Solve a quadratic that does not factor.

When the table finds only some of the zeros

The Rational Zero Theorem finds zeros that are whole numbers or fractions. Some polynomials also have zeros with square roots in them. In that case the lecture finds one rational zero, divides it out to leave a quadratic, and solves the quadratic with the quadratic formula. The last step is simplifying what the formula gives.

Simplifying a square root

Look for the largest perfect square that divides the number under the root. Split the root and take the square root of that part.

sqrt(20) = sqrt(4 * 5) = 2sqrt(5) the square root of 20 equals the square root of 4 times 5, end root, equals 2 the square root of 5 sqrt(12) = sqrt(4 * 3) = 2sqrt(3) the square root of 12 equals the square root of 4 times 3, end root, equals 2 the square root of 3 sqrt(18) = sqrt(9 * 2) = 3sqrt(2) the square root of 18 equals the square root of 9 times 2, end root, equals 3 the square root of 2

The perfect squares to check are 4, 9, 16, 25, and 36.

Dividing both terms

The quadratic formula ends with a fraction whose top has two terms. The bottom divides both of them.

(6 ± 2sqrt(5))/2 = 6/2 ± 2sqrt(5)/2 = 3 ± sqrt(5) open parenthesis 6 plus or minus 2 the square root of 5 close parenthesis over 2 equals 6 over 2 plus or minus 2 the square root of 5 over 2 equals 3 plus or minus the square root of 5

The common slip is to divide only one of the terms, or to cancel the 2 with the number under the root. The number under the root is not touched. Only the number in front of the root is divided.

Why both terms. A fraction bar divides everything above it. (6 + 2sqrt(5) 6 plus 2 the square root of 5 )/2 is the same as 6/2 + 2sqrt(5)/2, the way (6 + 4 6 plus 4 )/2 is the same as 6/2 + 4/2. You can check with a calculator: sqrt(5) the square root of 5 is about 2.236, so the top is about 10.472, and half of that is about 5.236. And 3 + 2.236 = 5.236 3 plus 2.236 equals 5.236 .
