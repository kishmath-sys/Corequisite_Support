2. Reading exponents

What you will be able to do

  • Read the power on x and on a constant.
  • Rewrite 1/x and sqrt(x) the square root of x as powers.
  • Find the largest power in an expression.

Terms, coefficients, and powers

A term is a number times a power of x, such as 7x^3. 7 x to the power 3 . The number in front, 7, is the coefficient. The small raised number, 3, is the exponent or power. It counts how many times x is used as a factor.

The powers you cannot see

  • x alone has power 1. x means x^1. x to the power 1 . So 5x is a term with power 1.
  • A plain number has power 0. Any nonzero number to the power 0 is 1, so 8 is the same as 8x^0. 8 x to the power 0 . A constant term has power 0.

Why x^0 x to the power 0 is 1. Follow the pattern downward. x^3 x cubed divided by x is x^2. x to the power 2 . x^2 x squared divided by x is x^1 x to the power 1 , which is x. x^1 x to the power 1 divided by x is x^0 x to the power 0 , and x divided by x is 1. So x^0 = 1 x to the power 0 equals 1 . Each step down in the exponent is one division by x.

Powers that are not whole numbers

Two rewrites from earlier courses show whether an expression really has whole-number powers. Keep going down the same pattern: one more division by x gives a negative exponent. And a root is a fractional exponent.

1/x = x^(-1) 1 over x equals x to the power negative 1, 1/x^2 = x^(-2) 1 over x squared equals x to the power negative 2, sqrt(x) = x^(1/2) the square root of x equals x to the power 1 over 2,

The whole numbers are 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on. A negative number or a fraction is not a whole number. So a term with x on the bottom of a fraction, or with x under a root, has a power that is not a whole number. The lecture uses exactly this test to decide whether a function is a polynomial.

The coefficient can be any real number

The restriction is only on the power. The coefficient may be a fraction, a decimal, a negative, or a number such as pi. In (1/2)x^4 open parenthesis 1 over 2 close parenthesis x to the power 4 the coefficient is 1/2 and the power is 4, a whole number. Do not confuse a fraction in front of x with x on the bottom of a fraction: (1/2)x is fine, and 1/(2x) is not.

The largest power

The lecture defines the degree as the largest power that appears. The skill is scanning every term and comparing powers, not coefficients and not position. In 4x^2 - 9x^5 + 1 4 x squared minus 9 x to the power 5 plus 1 the powers are 2, 5, and 0. The largest is 5, even though that term is not first and its coefficient is negative.

3. Factoring completely

What you will be able to do

  • Pull out a common factor first.
  • Factor trinomials and differences of squares.
  • Factor four terms by grouping, and keep going until nothing more factors.

What "completely" means

To find the zeros of a polynomial, the lecture first writes it as a product of the smallest possible factors. Factoring completely usually takes more than one step. Work through these checks in order, and after each step look again at what is left.

Check 1: a common factor

If every term shares a factor, pull it out front. For powers of x, the shared factor is the smallest power present.

x^3 + 6x^2 + 9x = x(x^2 + 6x + 9) x cubed plus 6 x squared plus 9 x equals x open parenthesis x squared plus 6 x plus 9 close parenthesis

Each term had at least one x, so one x came out. Divide each term by x to see what stays inside.

4. Solving a factored equation

What you will be able to do

  • Use the zero-factor property with several factors.
  • Handle a plain x, a number out front, and a repeated factor.
  • Read the power on each factor.

The zero-factor property with more than two factors

Once a polynomial is factored and set equal to zero, the zero-factor property finishes the job: if a product is zero, at least one factor is zero. It works for any number of factors. Set each factor that contains x equal to zero and solve.

(x + 2)(x - 3)(x + 3) = 0 open parenthesis x plus 2 close parenthesis open parenthesis x minus 3 close parenthesis open parenthesis x plus 3 close parenthesis equals 0 x + 2 = 0 or x - 3 = 0 or x + 3 = 0 x plus 2 equals 0 or x minus 3 equals 0 or x plus 3 equals 0 x = -2 or x = 3 or x = -3 x equals negative 2 or x equals 3 or x equals negative 3

The inputs that make a function equal zero are called its zeros.

Three special kinds of factor

  • A plain x. The factor x by itself is zero when x = 0 x equals 0 . This solution is easy to forget because there is nothing to solve.
  • A number out front. In 5(x - 2)(x + 7) = 0 5 open parenthesis x minus 2 close parenthesis open parenthesis x plus 7 close parenthesis equals 0 the 5 is a factor, but 5 is never zero. It gives no solution. Skip it.
  • A factor with a power. (x - 4)^2 = 0 open parenthesis x minus 4 close parenthesis squared equals 0 means (x - 4)(x - 4) = 0 open parenthesis x minus 4 close parenthesis open parenthesis x minus 4 close parenthesis equals 0 . Both copies give the same solution, x = 4 x equals 4 . So a repeated factor gives one solution, not two.
Why a squared factor gives one answer. (x - 4)^2 open parenthesis x minus 4 close parenthesis squared is zero only when x - 4 x minus 4 itself is zero, because the only number whose square is 0 is 0. Taking the square root of both sides shows it: x - 4 = ±sqrt(0) x minus 4 equals plus or minus the square root of 0 , and plus or minus zero is just zero. The exponent does not change where the zero is. The lecture uses the exponent for something else, which is how the graph behaves at that zero.

Keep track of the power on each factor

The lecture asks for the power on each factor alongside each zero. If a factor has no exponent written, its power is 1. Reading this off correctly is a matter of looking at the factored form before you solve.

5. Even and odd powers, crossing and touching

What you will be able to do

  • Tell even from odd.
  • Predict the sign of an even or odd power.
  • See crossing and touching on a graph.

Even and odd

An even number can be divided by 2 with nothing left over: 2, 4, 6, and so on. An odd number cannot: 1, 3, 5, and so on. The lecture sorts zeros by whether a power is even or odd. The reason comes from how powers treat negative numbers.

What an even power does to a negative

An even power multiplies an even number of copies. Negatives pair off and cancel, so the result is never negative.

(-3)^2 = 9 open parenthesis negative 3 close parenthesis squared equals 9 (3)^2 = 9 open parenthesis 3 close parenthesis squared equals 9 (-1)^4 = 1 open parenthesis negative 1 close parenthesis to the power 4 equals 1

What an odd power does to a negative

An odd power has one negative left over after pairing, so the sign of the input survives.

(-3)^1 = -3 open parenthesis negative 3 close parenthesis to the power 1 equals negative 3 (-2)^3 = -8 open parenthesis negative 2 close parenthesis cubed equals negative 8 (2)^3 = 8 open parenthesis 2 close parenthesis cubed equals 8
Why this decides the shape of the graph. Take the factor (x - 3) open parenthesis x minus 3 close parenthesis and try an input on each side of 3. At x = 2 x equals 2 , x - 3 x minus 3 is -1 negative 1 . At x = 4 x equals 4 , x - 3 x minus 3 is +1. So the factor changes sign as x passes 3. With an odd power the factor keeps that sign change, and the graph goes from one side of the x-axis to the other. With an even power, (x - 3)^2 open parenthesis x minus 3 close parenthesis squared is +1 on both sides. The sign does not change, so the graph comes to the axis and goes back the way it came.

Seeing it on a graph

At a point where a graph meets the x-axis, one of two things happens. The graph crosses: it passes from below the axis to above, or from above to below. Or the graph touches and turns: it comes to the axis, meets it at one point, and goes back to the side it came from.

On Graph A, look near x = -2 x equals negative 2 . The curve is below the axis on the left, reaches the axis, and goes back below. That is touching and turning. Near x = 1 x equals 1 the curve is below the axis on the left and above it on the right. That is crossing.

Smooth and unbroken

The lecture also describes polynomial graphs as smooth (rounded, with no sharp corners) and continuous (drawn without lifting the pencil). Graph A is both. A V shape, like the absolute value graph from Section 2.5, has a sharp corner. A graph with a gap or a jump is not continuous.
